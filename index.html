Post-Doctoral Researcher & Lecturer

I am a political scientist at Humboldt-Universität zu Berlin, where I work as a postdoctoral researcher and lecturer at Prof. Hanna Schwander's Chair of Political Sociology and Social Policy. Since July 2025, I also serve as Acting Managing Director of the Berlin Graduate School of Sciences.

I see climate change as the defining societal transformation and political challenge of our time. In my dissertation and beyond, I thus aim to understand how climate change reshapes citizens' relationship with democratic politics: whether growing climate concern erodes satisfaction with and support for democracy, how far citizens on both sides of the climate debate would set aside democratic principles to get their way, and how first-hand experiences such as water use restrictions and heat stress shape the way they judge the state.

Beyond climate change, I study how social change in citizens' everyday lives and communities shapes their political attitudes: for instance, how being able, or unable, to move affects people's political outlook, why some places become fertile ground for the radical right, and how increased visibility of gender and sexual diversity plays out politically.

What connects my work is a concern for democratic resilience and quality. This is why I also study questions of political representation, such as how voters respond to candidates' gender and how parties communicate with citizens with low literacy, as well as the conditions under which citizens support militant democracy. Methodologically, I mainly rely on original surveys, in part linked to structural data such as election results or regional statistics, as well as panel data and survey experiments. An overview of my current projects can be found here, and I am always open to collaboration.

My work has appeared in the Journal of European Public Policy, Politics & Governance and German Politics, among others, and further manuscripts are currently under review. A full list of my publications can be found in my CV.

The Underlying Structure of Political Support

Menzner, J. and D. Schweizer

Politics & Governance

Abstract

Contemporary politics is marked by concerns about declining citizen satisfaction with democracy, falling political trust, and weakening democratic support. Scholars in this field often rely on the concept of political support but use its indicators interchangeably or inconsistently. Meanwhile, despite its relevance for democratic resilience, studies on its underlying structure remain scarce. Drawing on the foundational work of Easton and Norris, we derive expectations about the dimensionality of political support and the interplay among its various indicators. We test these expectations using factor analysis, item response theory models, random intercept cross‐lagged panel models, and first‐difference models applied to 29 waves of the German Longitudinal Election Study panel. As expected, specific support fluctuates substantially, whereas diffuse support is more stable, even though recent data show small declines in multiple indicators. Our results indicate that political support indicators form a one‐dimensional latent trait. Along this trait, specific evaluations, like satisfaction with the government, differentiate respondents at higher levels of support, whereas more diffuse attitudes, such as institutional trust, best distinguish between those with below‐average support. Longitudinal analyses reveal no evidence that changes in specific support systematically precede changes in diffuse support. Instead, changes in any indicator tend to predict subsequent changes in others, indicating self‐reinforcing dynamics. Finally, first‐difference models offer limited support for a buffering “reservoir of goodwill.” Prior levels of specific support neither amplify nor mitigate the effects of subsequent changes on more diffuse indicators. Beyond structural insights, our item response theory results provide important practical guidance for researchers when deciding between political support indicators.

The Importance of Descriptive Representation & Cue-Taking in Free List Local Elections

Menzner, J., C. Schmid and L. Rettig

German Politics

Abstract

Analyzing the 2024 Mannheim municipal elections — where voters distributed 48 votes among 508 candidates on a free-list ballot — we find party affiliation, ballot position, and incumbency significantly shape vote totals, while gender and occupation cues show no direct effect. Candidates gain markedly from running in their own residential district and from an age profile matching that district's demographics.

Securing Moral Authority: A Cross-National Analysis of Protestant Engagement in the Implementation of Morality Policies in Western Europe

Mettang, O., E.-M. Euchner, L. Fischer and J. Menzner

VOLUNTAS

Abstract

Examining Protestant churches' involvement in four morality policy areas across Germany, England, and Denmark, we show that variation in religious engagement is driven mainly by churches' room to maneuver and policy congruence with state goals — governance capacity plays a secondary role. Providing social services remains a means through which churches can still exert moral authority.

The Potential of Open Lists for the Election of Women to the German Bundestag. Results of a Survey Experiment

Rudolph, L., Däubler, T. and J. Menzner

Politische Vierteljahresschrift  ·  1st Prize, Fritz-Thyssen-Foundation Award

Abstract

In a quota-representative survey experiment (N=2,640), we test whether German voters would choose female candidates from open party lists. Both female and male voters factor in candidate gender as expected, female candidates are rarely disadvantaged overall, and voters tend to level out gender-imbalanced lists — evidence that open lists merit more attention in electoral reform debates.

Subjective Freedom of Speech: Why Do Citizens Think They Cannot Speak Freely?

Menzner, J. and R. Traunmüller

Politische Vierteljahresschrift

Abstract

The first systematic study of subjective free speech in Germany, using a pre-registered analysis of GLES 2021 data. Three factors are significantly and consistently related to citizens feeling they cannot speak freely: political preferences, populist attitudes, and identification with the Alternative for Germany party.

Citizens' Two-Sided Demand for Undemocratic Climate Governance

Abstract

Using original German survey data (N=2,101) and two list experiments, I show that stronger climate preferences — for or against action — are robustly linked to support for non-democratic governance, including restricting activism, ignoring pro-climate court rulings, and censoring denial. 'True' support for undemocratic climate (in)action reaches roughly 35–39%, with skeptics least willing to disclose it openly.

Not Simply Simplified: Systematic Issue Emphasis in Easy-Read Manifestos

with Lukas Isermann & Julius Diener · presented MZES 2024, CompText 2023

Abstract

NLP analysis of 62 easy-read manifestos from five German parties shows welfare issues over-emphasised by 10 percentage points while economic issues are downplayed — parties appear to use easy-read manifestos for strategic gain rather than equal political participation.
